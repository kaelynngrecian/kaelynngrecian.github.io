“You got to understand how things work before you can fix them. Otherwise you’re taking potshots in the dark.”

- David Julius, Nobel Prize 2021

A ski trip at age 15 resulted in a deep laceration across my shin through an accident involving an unexpected fall. After the injury, I watched the blood pool beneath my feet as I kept waiting to be overcome with pain. As I stared at my leg, one question kept nagging at me: Why was I not feeling any pain? However, my question was answered once the ER physician explained how my body was temporarily blocking nerve signals through an overproduction of stress hormones such as adrenaline and cortisol. This intrigued me, as I had never previously considered what, if any, physiological pathways determine one’s sensitivity to pain. Furthermore, that curiosity only grew as I leaned in to observe the physician performing 20 internal sutures on my leg. Rather than being frightened or repulsed by my tissue, bone, muscle, and blood, I was energized by the realization that I still had so much to learn about my own body. As a result, this experience inspired me to find the answers to all of my remaining questions about the nervous system, as I was not satisfied with the simplified answers of being “in shock” that I was repeatedly given after my accident. Moreover, through my further reading about how sensory neurons function in tandem with higher brain centers to recognize and respond to pain signals, my initial curiosity developed into a desire to help others understand these processes. So, I became a high school tutor for chemistry, biology, and psychology, as I was eager to share my knowledge of these pathways with others who had not previously considered these intricacies. However, instead of solely teaching others about the science behind pain perception, I wanted to challenge myself to apply this knowledge to help patients better understand and manage their pain.

With these experiences shaping my interests, I plan to major in Neuroscience and Medical Anthropology at UNC–Chapel Hill due to my understanding that pain perception is mediated not only by the nervous system, but also by unique cultures and experiences. In addition to my studies, I plan to pursue research opportunities that explore pain perception and nervous system responses to various stimuli. In my research, I hope to gain a thorough understanding of how, and in what ways, pain perception is altered after physical trauma. With this knowledge of the regulatory processes involved in neural signaling as my foundation, I will be able to develop nuanced questions and pursue answers during my time in the lab. Through this process, I hope to strengthen my critical thinking and problem-solving skills by taking on complex problems that require thoughtful decisions.

Furthermore, I will be completing the NOLS Wilderness Emergency Medical Technician Training course over the summer of 2027 in Lander, Wyoming to earn both my EMT and Wilderness EMT Certifications. By completing this training, I will have the opportunity to exercise sound judgment while getting hands-on experience with practical skills in mock scenarios. Upon completing the course, I hope to apply my ability to think critically in high-pressure situations and work as an EMT at one of the many ski resorts near my home in Minnesota. Through this opportunity, I would be able to use my skills to provide care to those experiencing the fear and uncertainty that comes with a traumatic injury, a feeling I understand on a personal level.

Ultimately, I plan to draw on each of these distinct experiences as I work toward my eventual goal – becoming an inquisitive, driven, and capable physician.
